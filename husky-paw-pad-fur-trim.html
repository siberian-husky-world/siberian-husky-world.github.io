Should You Trim the Fur Between Your Husky's Paw Pads? Snowballs, Slippery Floors and the Muppet-Feet Debate

What the fur between the toes is for, when a flush trim fixes snowballs and slipping, why shaving backfires, and how to trim safely at home.

Published 2026-11-10

Trimming the fur between your Husky's paw pads sounds like a five-minute chore, yet it splits Husky owners into two loud camps: the "keep the muppet feet" crowd and the "tidy them flush" crowd. Most people only think about it after the first winter walk ends with a dog limping on golf balls of ice, or after a guest watches their Husky do the splits across the kitchen floor. Don't be the owner who finds out the hard way which camp was right for their own dog.

Quick answer: Yes, most pet Huskies benefit from a light trim, but not a shave. Cut the tufts that grow past the pads so the pads touch the ground and snow has less to grip, and leave the fur that sits down between the toes. The breed standard expects a Siberian's feet to be "well furred between the toes and pads", and trimming there is allowed for neatness. Shaving the paw bare removes insulation and leaves skin open to road salt. Trim every few weeks in winter or on slick floors, less often in a dry summer.

Does a Husky need the fur between its paw pads?

Yes. The fur between a Siberian's toes is part of its working design, and the official breed standard asks for it. The question is never "fur or no fur" but how far past the pads it should grow on a dog that lives indoors.

The FCI breed standard for the Siberian Husky describes both the front and hind feet the same way: medium in size, compact, "well furred between the toes and pads", with pads that are "tough and thickly cushioned". Siberians were bred to pull light loads over snow, and a compact, furred foot is what a sled dog stands on.

What the fur actually does

  • Insulation: fur between the toes keeps a layer of air around the skin when the dog stands on snow or cold ground.
  • Cushion for the skin between the pads: the skin in the gaps is thin and soft compared with the pads themselves.
  • Some protection on hot pavement: veterinarian Dr. Marty Becker notes on Vetstreet that the hair between the toes can also help protect pads from hot sidewalks in summer.

That is why "shave the feet" is the wrong reflex. A Husky's foot works best when the pads touch the ground and the fur stays tucked between them.

Is it allowed to trim a Siberian Husky's feet?

Yes. The breed standard explicitly allows trimming the fur between the toes and around the feet "to present a neater appearance". It is the only part of the coat, apart from the whiskers, where trimming is permitted.

The same FCI standard says trimming the fur on any other part of the dog "is not to be condoned and should be severely penalized", and lists "trimming of the coat, except as permitted above" as a fault. In other words, judges expect a natural Husky coat everywhere except the feet. Show breeders tidy feet all the time; it is not a pet-owner shortcut.

Trimming feet is not the same as shaving the coat

Owners sometimes confuse the two. Tidying the tufts under the paw has nothing to do with clipping the double coat, which this blog covers in detail in our wooly Husky grooming guide. You can leave the body coat completely natural and still keep neat feet.

What causes snowballs between a Husky's toes?

Snowballs form when wet snow sticks to long fur between and around the toes, partly melts from the dog's body heat and refreezes into hard lumps. The longer the tufts that hang past the pads, the more surface the snow has to grab.

The Ontario SPCA, which deals with long Canadian winters, advises owners of long-haired dogs to trim the hair between and around the paw pads. That, it says, minimizes painful ice balls, reduces the road salt and de-icing chemicals the feet pick up, and makes the paws easier to clean and balm after walks. Dr. Becker's advice on Vetstreet points the same way, with one important limit: trim the hair between the toes to reduce ice balls, "but leave a little hair for insulation and traction".

Why Huskies still get snowballs

Huskies are built for snow, so owners are often surprised. Two things usually explain it:

  • Wet, sticky snow: powder at very low temperatures tends to brush off, while slushy snow near freezing clumps on any fur.
  • Overgrown pet feet: a working dog wears its foot fur down on trails, while a pet that walks on sidewalks and carpets grows long tufts that nobody trims.

Booties do not always fix it

Booties keep salt off the pads, but in deep snow they can move the problem upward. A New York groomer quoted by PetMD notes that when snow is more than an inch thick, snowballs generally form above the booties, so warm soapy water remains the main fix. If your Husky pulls a sled or runs on snow trails, our musher's glossary explains what mushers mean by booties and other gear.

Will trimming paw fur stop a Husky slipping on floors?

Often it helps, because fur that covers the pads acts like a sock on hardwood, laminate and tile. Trimming lets the rubbery pads grip the floor again. It is not a full fix for a dog that slides because of weak back legs, though, so rugs still matter.

In a busy r/husky thread asking exactly this question, the top answers came from owners of senior Huskies. One wrote that their 13-year-old slides on hardwood and walks better after a pad trim; another trims an older dog "specifically to help prevent wood floor sliding"; a commenter who works in a grooming salon called traction one of the main reasons to keep the feet trimmed. Others pushed back: one said a trim "won't help with the slipping" and suggested rugs instead, and several described laying cheap yoga mats or runners along the dog's usual paths.

When traction is the real reason to trim

  • Your floors are mostly hard surfaces and your Husky slides when it turns or runs to the door.
  • Fur visibly covers part of the pads when you look from below.
  • Your dog is older or less steady than it used to be.

If the fur already ends level with the pads and the dog still slips, more cutting will not help. Look at rugs, nails and how the dog moves, and talk to your vet if a senior Husky is suddenly unsteady.

The muppet-feet debate: trim or leave them natural?

Both camps have a point, and neither is "the bad owner". The fluffy look is charming and the fur is useful; the tidy foot grips better and collects less ice and dirt. The sensible middle is a flush trim: cut what hangs past the pads, keep what sits between them.

How often should you trim Husky paw fur?

For most pet Huskies, a quick flush trim every three to six weeks during the snowy months and on slick indoor floors is enough, with longer gaps in a dry summer. Let the fur, not the calendar, decide: trim when it starts to hang past the pads again.

Season by season

  • Late fall: do a flush trim before the first wet snow, so you are not working on frozen, matted tufts.
  • Winter: check the feet after every snowy walk; trim when tufts grow past the pads again. Keep a towel and a bowl of warm water by the door.
  • Spring: mud and burrs collect in long foot fur; a trim makes after-walk wipes quicker. Coat blow season is also busy, so see our Husky shedding season timeline to plan both.
  • Summer: trim less. The fur helps a little against hot pavement, and there is no snow to ball up.

Very active dogs that run on trails or pull often wear their foot fur down on their own. A Husky that gets the daily work described in our Husky exercise guide may need fewer trims than a mostly indoor dog.

How to trim the fur between your Husky's paw pads, step by step

Work slowly, in good light, with the dog lying down. The goal is fur level with the pads, not bare skin.

What you need

  • Small round-tipped (blunt) scissors, or a small cordless trimmer made for paws
  • A slicker brush or comb
  • Treats and, ideally, a helper
  • Good light, or a headlamp
  • A towel

The routine

  1. Start with dry, clean feet. Wet or muddy fur clumps and hides the skin. If there are snowballs, melt them with warm water and dry the foot first; never cut a frozen lump out.
  2. Brush the foot fur out. A quick pass with a slicker or comb lifts the tufts so you can see where they end. Our FURminator vs rake vs slicker guide explains which brush suits which job.
  3. Hold the paw and spread it gently. Press lightly on the top of the foot so the pads open. Keep sessions short with a dog that is still learning.
  4. Trim level with the pads. Hold the scissors flat, parallel to the pads, and cut only what sticks out past them. Never point the tips down into the gaps between the pads.
  5. Check with your fingertip. Run a finger across the bottom of the paw. You should feel pads, with fur ending at the same height, not tufts brushing your finger.
  6. Tidy the outline if you like. Comb the fur on top of the foot down and trim around the edge of the toes for a neat, round foot, as the breed standard allows.
  7. Repeat on all four feet, then reward. Back feet are usually harder; the video above shows one way to handle them.

When to hand it to a groomer

An expert quoted in the PetMD guide advises owners who are not comfortable, or whose dog will not hold still, to trim around the feet but leave the pads to a professional, because the skin there is delicate and easy to nick. If your Husky fights, mouths or panics when you hold a paw, book a groomer and work on paw handling with treats between visits.

Common mistakes when trimming Husky paw fur

  • Shaving the paw bare. It removes insulation and exposes skin to salt and cold. Flush with the pads is enough.
  • Digging the scissor tips between the pads. The skin in the gaps is thin. Cut parallel to the pads, never into them.
  • Cutting a snowball out. Ice pulls the fur tight against the skin, so scissors can easily catch it. Melt the ice with warm water first.
  • Trimming a struggling dog. A sudden kick and sharp scissors are a bad mix. Short sessions, a helper, or a groomer.
  • Expecting a trim to fix every slip. If the fur is already flush and your dog still slides, add rugs and runners and ask your vet about an older dog's steadiness.
  • Forgetting the after-walk wipe. Trimmed or not, rinse and dry the feet after salted sidewalks, including between the toes.
  • Trimming because the internet said so. A Husky on carpet in a dry climate with fur that ends at the pads may not need trimming at all. Look at your own dog's feet first.

Husky paw fur FAQ

Should I trim the fur between my Husky's paw pads?

For most pet Huskies, yes, lightly. Trim the tufts that grow past the pads so the pads touch the ground and wet snow has less to stick to, but leave the fur between the toes. The breed standard expects well-furred feet and allows trimming there for neatness.

Is it bad to shave a Husky's paws?

Shaving the paw bare is usually a bad idea. The fur between the toes insulates the foot, and a groomer quoted by PetMD warns that shaved paws leave bare skin exposed to road salt, which irritates it and makes dogs lick their feet. A flush trim gives most of the benefit without that downside.

How do I get snowballs out of my Husky's paws?

Melt them. Dip the foot in a bowl of warm water or hold a warm, wet washcloth on it until the ice softens, then dry the foot well, including between the toes. Do not cut a frozen snowball out with scissors, because the ice pulls the fur tight against the skin.

Will trimming paw fur stop my Husky slipping on hardwood?

It often helps when fur covers part of the pads, because the pads can grip the floor again. If the fur already ends level with the pads and your dog still slides, add rugs or runners along its usual paths and ask your vet if an older dog is becoming unsteady.

Should I use scissors or clippers on my Husky's feet?

Either works. Small round-tipped scissors are easy for owners to control on a calm dog; a small paw trimmer is quicker on a wiggly one. Whatever you use, keep it flat and parallel to the pads and never point it into the gaps between them.

Does trimming between the toes affect a Husky in dog shows?

No, as long as you only trim the feet. The breed standard allows trimming the fur between the toes and around the feet for a neater look, and penalizes trimming anywhere else on the coat.

Neat feet, not bare feet

You do not have to choose between muppet feet and shaved feet. Trimming the fur between your Husky's paw pads so it ends level with the pads gives you fewer snowballs, better grip on hard floors and quicker after-walk cleanups, while keeping the fur that a sled dog's foot was built around. Check the feet from below, trim when the tufts hang past the pads, never shave, and let a groomer take over if your dog will not hold still.
