Is the FURminator Bad for Huskies? Blade Deshedders vs Undercoat Combs and Slicker Brushes Compared

What each tool actually touches in a double coat, and how Husky people keep the guard hairs that make the breed look like itself.

Published 2026-11-06

Whether the FURminator is bad for Huskies is one of the oldest arguments in Husky circles, and it usually starts the first spring a new owner finds tufts of undercoat drifting across the kitchen floor. If you are standing in the grooming aisle holding a blade deshedder in one hand and an undercoat rake in the other, you are in very good company. Most of us who live with Sibes have owned both, and many of us have retired one of them to the back of a drawer.

Quick answer: The FURminator is not automatically bad for a Husky, but it is the least forgiving tool in the kit. Its fine stainless-steel edge can snag and break the long guard hairs if it is pressed hard, used often, or dragged through a coat that is not blowing. An undercoat rake or comb and a slicker or pin brush do most Husky coat work with less risk to the outer coat; a blade deshedder, if you use one at all, belongs to light, short sessions during a real coat blow.

The blade itself is not the villain in most Husky stories. The way it is used is. The same tool that lifts out a cloud of dead undercoat in five gentle minutes can, in a tired owner's hand on a Sunday afternoon, keep pulling until the shiny outer layer starts to look thin and choppy.

Is the FURminator actually bad for a Husky's coat?

Not by design, but it can be by habit. The manufacturer says the edge removes only loose undercoat, while a long line of Husky owners report broken guard hairs after heavy use, which points to technique and frequency as the real dividing line.

The FURminator guide on whether the tool damages fur recommends using it once or twice a week for 10 to 20 minutes, only on a completely dry coat, gliding the stainless-steel edge in the direction of hair growth without excessive pressure. The same page tells owners to stop immediately if they notice redness, and says the tool should not be used on breeds without an undercoat or with sensitive skin. Those are the brand's own guardrails, and they are tighter than many people assume when they buy the tool.

Why Husky people are more cautious than the label

The manufacturer states that the topcoat stays intact. Owners who have watched their own dogs for a few seasons are less certain. On the Husky Owners forum, one member wrote in a 2009 thread about the FURminator that it cut their dog's top coat and stripped a lot of his color, and that the guard hairs took a long time to grow back. Other members in the same thread loved the tool. That split is exactly what you hear at dog parks and sled-dog meets today: some dogs and some hands get on with a blade, others do not.

Treat these as experiences from people like you, not a controlled study. Nobody has measured guard hair breakage across hundreds of Huskies with different tools, so the honest position is that a blade carries more risk to the outer coat than a rake or brush, and the size of that risk depends on how you hold it.

How do blade deshedders, undercoat combs and slicker brushes differ?

A blade deshedder cuts through the coat with a row of fine teeth on a straight edge, an undercoat rake or comb drags loose fluff out with separated pins, and a slicker brush detangles and smooths the surface with fine bent wires. They touch the two layers of a Husky coat very differently.

The edge versus the pin

The difference that matters most is shape. A pin has a rounded tip and space around it, so a long guard hair can slip through while the loose fluff behind it is pulled out. A blade is a continuous edge. Anything that lodges between its teeth, whether loose undercoat or a still-anchored guard hair, is pulled along with the same force. That is why the same pressure that feels harmless with a rake can be too much with a blade.

Where a slicker brush fits

A slicker brush rarely gets enough credit in Husky groups. It will not empty a blowing coat on its own, but it tidies the surface after the rake, catches the last fluff and leaves the guard hairs lying flat. Think of it as the finishing tool that makes your dog look groomed rather than raked.

What does a Husky coat need from any grooming tool?

It needs the dense undercoat thinned out when it loosens and the guard hairs left whole. Both layers are part of what the breed is supposed to be, so a good tool removes dead fluff without changing the outline of the coat.

The Siberian Husky Club of America's breed standard, approved in 2021, describes a double coat of medium length with a soft, dense undercoat of sufficient length to support the outer coat, and guard hairs that are straight and somewhat smooth-lying. It also notes that the absence of the undercoat during the shedding season is normal, and that trimming fur anywhere except the whiskers and around the feet is not to be condoned and should be severely penalized. For show people, a coat thinned by a cutting edge is a fault; for pet owners, it is simply not the coat the dog was built to wear.

The Wikipedia entry on the Siberian Husky sums up the same structure: a double coat thicker than that of most breeds, with a dense, finely wavy undercoat and a longer topcoat of thicker, straight guard hairs. Once you can tell those two hair types apart in your brush, you can judge any tool yourself.

The hair-in-the-tool test

Pull the hair out of your tool after a few strokes and lay it on a dark surface. Soft, crimped, pale fluff is undercoat; that is what you want. Long, straight, glossy hairs are guard hairs. A few usually come out with any tool. A growing proportion of them, or short hairs with blunt ends, means the tool or your pressure is taking more than loose coat.

When does a blade deshedder make sense for a Husky?

Mostly during a full coat blow, after a rake has already done the heavy lifting, and in short, light sessions. Outside of a blow there is very little loose undercoat for a blade to find, so it is more likely to catch what should stay.

Our Husky shedding season timeline walks through when the undercoat typically lets go in spring and fall. That window is when many experienced owners allow a blade back into the routine, and even then mainly for stubborn patches on the hindquarters and pants once the rake stops pulling much out.

A sensible place for the blade

  • After the rake, not instead of it. Use the rake or comb until it returns only small amounts, then decide whether any finish is needed.
  • On dry coat only. The manufacturer's own advice is a completely dry coat; damp hair clumps and drags.
  • With the weight of the tool. If you are pushing, the blade is the wrong tool for that spot today.
  • Within the label limits. One or two sessions a week of 10 to 20 minutes is the brand's ceiling, not a target.

When to leave it in the drawer

Skip the blade on puppies with soft coats, on a Husky between blows, over a recently clipped or very thin area, and on any patch where you have already seen redness. If the coat is matted, a blade is the wrong answer; work the tangle loose with a comb or ask a groomer who knows double coats.

What are Husky owners saying after years with these tools?

The pattern in owner communities is consistent: many tried a blade first, many moved to a rake, and the ones who kept the blade use it lightly and rarely. The rake is the tool most often handed down from one owner to the next.

In a 2012 Husky Owners thread asking whether the FURminator is worth the money, one member said they prefer an undercoat rake for their Huskies and that the dogs complain much less than they did with the blade. Another said they got rid of theirs because it took out too much guard hair. That is not proof, but it is the shared memory of a community that brushes out a lot of undercoat every year, and it lines up with what the tool shapes would predict.

What groomers add

Groomers who specialize in double coats tend to talk less about the tool and more about method. The Primpaws video above is a good example: the work is done in lifted sections with a rake, a pin brush and a comb, and the groomer checks progress as she goes. Line brushing takes longer than raking the surface, but it reaches the whole coat, which is what removes the most loose hair.

Grooming as shared time

Many owners say the best grooming sessions are the calm ones after a good run. If your dog is still buzzing, a session right after the outing ideas in our guide to how much exercise a Siberian Husky needs often goes more smoothly than one squeezed in before the walk. A dog who settles for brushing also gives you time to look at what the tool is pulling out.

Step by step: a coat-friendly deshedding session

This is the order many Husky households settle on. Adjust it to your dog's tolerance and your space; the goal is loose undercoat out and guard hairs left in place.

  1. Set up where hair can fly. Outside, a garage or a tiled floor saves your sofa. Have a bag ready for the fluff.
  2. Check that the coat is dry. After a bath, rain or a swim, wait until the coat is completely dry before using a blade at all.
  3. Run a wide-tooth comb first. Look for tangles behind the ears, in the pants and at the base of the tail, and loosen them gently by hand or comb.
  4. Rake with the grain, section by section. Start at the neck and work back, giving extra time to the hindquarters and back legs where undercoat tends to pack in.
  5. Line brush stubborn areas. Lift a section against the grain with one hand and brush it down with a pin or slicker brush, then move to the next line.
  6. Do the hair-in-the-tool test. If long, straight guard hairs start to outnumber the soft fluff, change tools or stop for the day.
  7. Finish lightly, if at all, with the blade. A few gentle passes on patches the rake could not shift, using the weight of the tool and staying inside the brand's 10 to 20 minute session limit.
  8. Smooth the outer coat. A slicker or pin brush over the top lays the guard hairs flat and catches the last fluff.
  9. Stop before your dog does. End while your Husky is still relaxed, with a treat or a game, so the next session starts on a good note.

A dog who has been digging resting pits in the garden will carry soil in the undercoat; if that sounds familiar, our piece on why Huskies dig holes in the yard explains the habit, and a quick comb-out of loose dirt before raking keeps your tools cleaner.

Common mistakes with deshedding tools

Almost every damaged coat story includes one of these. None of them requires buying anything new to fix.

  • Using the blade as the main tool. It removes hair quickly, which makes it tempting to keep going. Let the rake carry the load.
  • Grooming a blade over the same patch. Long sessions on the rump or neck are where owners most often notice thin, dull or lighter areas.
  • Pressing harder when less hair comes out. Less hair usually means less loose undercoat, not a reason to push.
  • Brushing a damp coat. Wet undercoat clumps and drags; the manufacturer limits use to a completely dry coat.
  • Deshedding all year at blow-season intensity. Between blows, a weekly comb and brush is usually plenty.
  • Shaving or clipping to save brushing. The breed standard treats trimming beyond whiskers and feet as a serious fault, and a clipped double coat changes the dog's natural outline.
  • Ignoring what is in the tool. The hair you pull out tells you more about your technique than any product claim.

Frequently asked questions

Does the FURminator cut guard hairs on a Husky?

The manufacturer says its edge removes loose undercoat and leaves the topcoat intact. Several Husky owners report the opposite after heavy use, describing cut or broken guard hairs that took a long time to grow back. Light pressure, short sessions and use only during a coat blow keep the risk lowest.

Is an undercoat rake better than a FURminator for Huskies?

For most Husky owners, yes, as the main tool. A rake's rounded, separated pins pull loose undercoat while letting most guard hairs slide past. A blade can be a light finishing tool during a heavy blow, but many owners stop needing it once they learn to rake and line brush.

How often can I use a FURminator on my Husky?

The brand's own guidance is once or twice a week for 10 to 20 minutes on a completely dry coat. Treat that as a ceiling. During light shedding between blows, most Huskies need no blade work at all, just a comb and brush.

Do I need a slicker brush if I already have a rake?

It helps. The rake empties the undercoat, while a slicker or pin brush detangles and smooths the outer coat so the guard hairs lie flat. Many owners use the rake first and the slicker last in the same session.

How can I tell if a tool is removing too much coat?

Look at the hair in the tool. Soft, crimped, pale fluff is undercoat. Long, straight, glossy hairs are guard hairs; a few are normal, but a growing share of them, or short blunt-ended hairs, means you should switch to a gentler tool or lighten your pressure.

Should I shave my Husky instead of deshedding?

The Siberian Husky breed standard permits trimming only of the whiskers and the fur between the toes and around the feet, and treats other trimming as a serious fault. Regular raking and brushing during the coat blow keeps the natural double coat in place.

Keeping the coat that makes a Husky a Husky

The FURminator is not banned in most Husky households, but it has been demoted. The undercoat rake and comb do the heavy work, the slicker or pin brush finishes the surface, and the blade, if it stays in the kit at all, comes out for a few light minutes during a real coat blow. Keep checking what your tools pull out, stay within the label limits, and your Husky keeps the straight, smooth-lying guard hairs that the breed is known for.
